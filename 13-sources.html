The source nobody here has read

Edward Lengel’s note 16, which is what led this research to the 317th’s own regimental history, cites two works. One of them — Craighill — was found, read, and is used throughout these pages. The other has never been consulted:

WorkWhy it matters
Jennings C. Wise, The Great Crusade, p. 195A contemporary or near-contemporary account of the 80th Division’s Meuse-Argonne fighting, cited by a modern historian for the very attack in which David Bryant was wounded. It is the highest-value unread source in this project.

A caution for anyone searching after us

There is a second Bryant in the 317th Infantry Neuman Bryant, Private 1st Class, of Kieffer, West Virginia — a cook in Company G, and no relation. He appears twice in that company’s published diary.

He is in a different battalion and a different state, but any index search for “Bryant, 317th Infantry” will return him. Check the company and the home state before accepting a match.

Still unproven

Do not repeat these as fact
  • The gas. The author’s own recollection, aged twelve, of being told so by David Bryant himself. Not supported by his 1922 form, which records the gunshot wound alone. Strengthened but unproven: gas is documented on that ground on that day, but named on no one.
  • Walter’s death, 22 Sep 1945 at Brookneal, is stated from family knowledge and is not yet documented. His birth is Tier 1 from his own signed card.
  • The line of descent Walter → Virginia Mae (“Ma Mae”) → the author’s mother → the author is stated by him from family knowledge and not yet cited from vital records.
  • The date of the visit to Chase City, given as about 1970, rests on two childhood memories and no document.
  • Whether David ever spoke of Alexander to anyone. Two witnesses establish that he did not do so on one afternoon. Nothing establishes more than that.
  • The pre-war Virginia company all three brothers served in. No card names the unit.
  • The officer who left Company L to take the battalion and then the regiment. Capt. John W. Woodend is the leading candidate, not a confirmed identification, and is not named as such in the narrative.
  • Whether William Kokos suffered what would now be called PTSD. Asserted in the family account; not a diagnosis anyone recorded in 1918 or 1958.

A note on the author

This account is compiled by John Gladin, great-grand-nephew of David Harrison Bryant and Alexander Dudley Bryant, and great-grandson of their brother Walter Newton Bryant. The visit to Chase City described in the first and last chapters is his own memory, made when he was about twelve.

He retired as a Lieutenant Colonel, Georgia State Department of Defense. He was trained as an infantry officer, and completed his advanced officer course — Amphibious Warfare School, Marine Corps University — in 1995.

Why that is recorded here rather than on the first page The passages in this account describing what sergeants actually do — who holds the roster, who runs the platoon, what happens to a company when its officers are hit — are not assembled from reading. They come from having been an infantry officer and having watched the same arrangements work, eighty years later and with better radios.

That is worth a reader knowing. It is put at the back rather than the front because the story opens with a boy of twelve who knew none of it, and rank on that page would be the wrong voice.

A note on the images

All document images are photographs of original records, with two exceptions, both labelled wherever they appear: the terrain map is modern, and the restored portrait of Alexander in the companion account is an AI reconstruction and is not evidence of his appearance.

The Signal Corps photograph of Châtel-Chéhéry is a U.S. Government work and in the public domain. Page images from Lengel are reproduced for research and commentary and are not relicensed.
