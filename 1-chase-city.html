One · Before any of it

Chase City and the old home place

A family that came up out of the Carolina foothills, a firm with the father’s name on it, and a house with two porches.

Jodie remembered the house before she remembered the man.

I remember being very impressed with the family home — a big two story house with a porch across the front upstairs & downstairs. It sat back off the road behind a fence & a pasture and I recall it being in town. Jodie Sweat, 22 August 2026

They called it the old home place. In about 1970 it was a stop on a family reunion outing — something to be shown to children. Fifty-two years before that it was simply where the Bryants lived, and where two of the three sons of the house had last slept before they went to France.

Out of Caldwell County

All three brothers were born in Caldwell County, North Carolina, in the foothills below the Blue Ridge. Walter and David were born at or near Lenoir, the county seat; Alexander at Rufus, a community in the same county. Walter in 1888, David in 1894, Alexander in 1895 — the two younger boys nineteen months apart.

By 1917 the whole family was at Chase City, Mecklenburg County, Virginia, in the Southside tobacco country a hundred and eighty miles north-east. Their mother was Nancy E. Moore. Their father was William Joseph Bryant, and he had a business.

W. J. Bryant & Sons

The firm’s name is written on the draft cards in the brothers’ own hands, under By whom employed? Walter gave his trade as farming and saw milling. Alexander gave W. J. Bryant & Sons as his employer outright.

The name of the family firm was the family. & Sons — there were three of them, and their father had put them in the title of his business before anyone had heard of Châtel-Chéhéry.

It is worth being plain about what that meant in 1917. Farming and saw-milling in Southside Virginia was not a trade a man walked away from lightly. It fed people. It was also, in the language of the draft, an occupation that could excuse a man from service — and all three brothers said so on their cards.

Soldiers already

There is one more thing the three cards agree on, and it took the discovery of the third card to see it. Each of these men had already served in the Virginia state troops before the war began:

BrotherPrior service, as stated on his own card
Walter, 28Private, Infantry — 2½ years, Va. State Guards
David, 23Corporal, Infantry — about three years, Virginia
Alexander, 21Private, Infantry — about three years, Virginia

Three brothers of one household, all three in the state troops, one of them already wearing a corporal’s stripes. It explains a good deal about what happened to them later — why David was a sergeant fifteen days after induction, and why Alexander was already a sergeant when he sailed. It also opens a question this research has not yet answered: which company? Neither card names the unit. Somewhere there is a Southside Virginia roster with all three of these names on it, and the names of every neighbour who drilled beside them.

Still open The pre-war Virginia company has not been identified. It is the single most promising unfound document in this project: it would place all three brothers in one room, by name, before the war that separated them.
